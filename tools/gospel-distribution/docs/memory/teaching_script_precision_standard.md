## The Precision Standard for Teaching Scripts

**This is the baseline for all future teaching work.** Not negotiable.

### Core Principle

Write like you're explaining something you genuinely understand to someone you respect. No word is decorative. Every sentence earns its place.

### Six Rules

**1. Everyday language**
- Use words a real person uses when explaining something they understand
- Not "That's proof" or "That's not random" — decorative and vague
- But "That shows you what's blocking you" or "That's the reason" — precise and clear

**2. Every claim must be true and defensible**
- Never make a vague claim to avoid being wrong
- Vague writing is not safe—it's useless
- The goal is the strongest defensible claim, not the most comfortable one
- If you state it, you must be able to defend it

**3. Every claim must be precise**
- As specific as it can be without becoming false
- "God won't move where there's unforgiveness" is precise (true, defensible)
- "God can't bless that" is vague (too broad, harder to defend)
- "God can't work where there's unforgiveness" is more precise than "God struggles with bitterness"

**4. Useful, not persuasive or inspiring**
- Your job is to bring them to the truth and allow them to reason for themselves
- Not to convince them, but to show them so they can see it
- They do the work of understanding—you provide the clarity

**5. Complex ideas in plain sentences**
- Depth doesn't require complexity
- Short sentences land harder than long ones
- Use them at the moment of impact
- Example: "God won't move where there's unforgiveness. He can't. Not because He doesn't want to. But because you've closed the door."

**6. No decorative words**
- Every word works
- Remove: "You know what's really happening?" (filler)
- Remove: "Think about that for a second" (filler)
- Remove: "Let me show you..." (unnecessary bridge)
- Keep only what advances the reasoning

### Structure That Serves This Standard

Each teaching moves through logic and reasoning, thinking out loud, so people have something to meditate on—not just bare facts.

**Bad version (bare facts):**
"Your breakthrough is blocked because you won't forgive. That's not random. That's proof."

**Good version (reasoning, meditatable):**
"Your breakthrough is blocked because you won't forgive. That's the reason. You're asking God to change your situation. But you're holding onto what they did. God won't move where there's unforgiveness. He can't. Not because He doesn't want to. But because you've closed the door. So your situation stays the same. That shows you what's blocking you."

### Scriptural Foundation

Scripture stays front and center. It's not decoration—it's the proof. Matthew 6:14-15 isn't "one example." It's the foundation that makes the claim defensible.

### How to Apply This

Before finalizing any teaching script:

- [ ] Read it aloud. Does it sound like a real person explaining something they understand?
- [ ] Check every claim. Can you defend it? Is it precise or vague?
- [ ] Look for filler words and phrases. Remove them.
- [ ] Is the reasoning visible? Can someone follow your thinking and meditate on it?
- [ ] Does every word earn its place? Can anything be cut without losing meaning?
- [ ] Is it scripturally sound? Does it hold up under scrutiny?

### Quality Checklist (All Standards Met)

✓ **Everyday language** — sounds like a real person, not performed
✓ **Defensible claims** — true and specific, not vague
✓ **Precise not comfortable** — strongest version, not safest
✓ **Useful not persuasive** — brings truth, lets them reason
✓ **Complex ideas plainly** — depth in short sentences
✓ **Every word earns its place** — no decoration
✓ **Meditatable** — something to sit with, not just information
